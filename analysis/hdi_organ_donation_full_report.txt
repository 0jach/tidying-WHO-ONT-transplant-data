================================================================================
 HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX, ORGAN DONATION, AND TRANSPLANT EFFICIENCY
 A cross-national analysis of 122 countries (2000-2023)
================================================================================

 Prepared by Matteo Colombo
 Internal reference for paper writing


1. DATA SOURCES AND METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

1.1 Datasets

Three datasets from the WHO Global Observatory on Donation and Transplantation:

  - donors_tidy_total.csv (9,288 rows): annual counts of actual and utilised
    deceased donors by country, covering 194 countries from 2000 to 2024.

  - donors_tidy.csv (5,755 rows): a more granular version splitting donors by
    type (DBD: donation after brain death, DCD: donation after circulatory
    death) and providing both total actual and total utilised counts per row.
    This dataset was used for efficiency calculations.

  - transplant_tidy.csv (46,440 rows): annual transplant counts by country,
    organ type (kidney, liver, lung, pancreas, kidney-pancreas, small bowel),
    and donor source (living vs. deceased).

The fourth dataset is the UNDP Human Development Index (6,604 rows), sourced
from Our World in Data. It provides HDI values for 215 entities from 1990 to
2023. The HDI is a composite index measuring average achievement in three basic
dimensions of human development: a long and healthy life (life expectancy),
knowledge (mean and expected years of schooling), and a decent standard of
living (GNI per capita).

1.2 Country name harmonisation

The WHO and UNDP datasets use different naming conventions for 21 countries
(e.g., "Bolivia (Plurinational State of)" vs. "Bolivia", "Republic of Korea"
vs. "South Korea"). A mapping table was created to harmonise all names. After
harmonisation, 122 countries were matched for the HDI vs. donation/transplant
analysis, and 88 countries with active deceased donor programmes were matched
for the efficiency analysis.

1.3 Key definitions

  - Donors per million population (PMP): total actual deceased donors divided
    by population (in millions).
  - Transplants per million population (PMP): total transplants (all organs,
    all sources) divided by population.
  - Utilisation efficiency: ratio of utilised deceased donors to total actual
    deceased donors (Utilised DD / Actual DD). A value of 1.0 means every
    recovered donor led to at least one transplant.
  - HDI tiers: Low (< 0.55), Medium (0.55-0.70), High (0.70-0.80),
    Very High (0.80+). These thresholds follow the UNDP classification.

1.4 Statistical methods

Two correlation measures were used throughout: Pearson r (linear correlation,
sensitive to outliers) and Spearman rho (rank correlation, robust to outliers
and capable of capturing non-linear monotonic relationships). Where Spearman is
notably higher than Pearson, the relationship is likely non-linear. A simple
ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was used to compute residuals for the
overperformer/underperformer analysis. All p-values are two-tailed.


2. FINDING 1: HDI IS A STRONG PREDICTOR OF ORGAN DONATION CAPACITY
================================================================================

The central finding is that the Human Development Index is strongly and
significantly correlated with both deceased donor rates and transplant rates
per million population.

2.1 Correlation coefficients

  Measure                 | Pearson r | p-value   | Spearman rho | p-value
  -------------------------+-----------+-----------+--------------+----------
  Donors PMP vs HDI       |  0.62     | < 0.001   |  0.75        | < 0.001
  Transplants PMP vs HDI  |  0.68     | < 0.001   |  0.77        | < 0.001

The gap between Pearson and Spearman values indicates a non-linear
relationship: donation rates accelerate past a development threshold (roughly
HDI 0.80) rather than climbing at a constant rate. This is consistent with the
idea that organ donation requires a minimum level of medical infrastructure,
legal frameworks, and public trust that only materialises above a certain
development level.

2.2 Rates by HDI tier

  HDI tier           | Median donors PMP | Median transplants PMP
  --------------------+-------------------+-----------------------
  Low (< 0.55)       |  ~0               |  0.5
  Medium (0.55-0.70) |  ~0               |  3.3
  High (0.70-0.80)   |  1.7              |  8.9
  Very High (0.80+)  |  13.5             |  41.5

Countries with very high HDI have roughly 25 times the transplant rate of
medium-HDI countries. The jump from High to Very High is itself nearly
fivefold, suggesting that the last stretch of development is where donation
systems truly scale.

2.3 Linear model

A simple OLS regression yields: donors_pmp = 51.3 x HDI - 32.2 (R^2 = 0.39).
HDI alone explains about 39% of the variance in donor rates. The remaining 61%
is driven by policy, culture, and institutional factors (see Finding 4).


3. FINDING 2: THE LIVING-TO-DECEASED DONOR TRANSITION
================================================================================

One of the most structurally revealing patterns is the shift from living to
deceased donation as development increases. This is not merely a statistical
artefact -- it reflects fundamental differences in healthcare infrastructure,
legal frameworks, and medical capacity.

  HDI tier    | Deceased (%) | Living (%) | Dominant source
  ------------+--------------+------------+-------------------------
  Low         |  0.5%        | 99.5%      | Almost entirely living
  Medium      | 28.6%        | 71.4%      | Mostly living
  High        | 68.4%        | 31.6%      | Mostly deceased
  Very High   | 74.1%        | 25.7%      | Predominantly deceased

Why this matters:

Deceased donation requires a chain of capabilities that living donation does
not: brain-death legislation, intensive care infrastructure, organ procurement
organisations (OPOs), and transplant coordination systems. These are expensive
and complex to build.

The critical advantage of deceased donation is multi-organ retrieval: a single
deceased donor can provide two kidneys, a liver, two lungs, a heart, a
pancreas, and intestines -- potentially saving or improving up to eight lives.
A living donor can typically donate only one kidney or a liver segment. This is
why transplant volumes scale so much faster once deceased donation
infrastructure is in place.


4. FINDING 3: ORGAN DIVERSITY INCREASES WITH DEVELOPMENT
================================================================================

The composition of transplanted organs changes dramatically across HDI tiers.

  HDI tier    | Kidney | Liver | Lung  | Pancreas | Other
  ------------+--------+-------+-------+----------+------
  Low         | 92%    |  8%   |  0%   |  0%      |  0%
  Medium      | 81%    | 19%   |  0.5% |  0.1%    |  0.1%
  High        | 75%    | 22%   |  1.2% |  0.7%    |  0.5%
  Very High   | 66%    | 26%   |  3.7% |  2.2%    |  1.9%

Kidneys dominate in low-HDI settings for two reinforcing reasons. First, kidney
transplantation from living donors is technically the simplest major organ
transplant. Second, in the absence of deceased donation, kidneys are
essentially the only organ available.

Lung and pancreas transplants only appear at meaningful levels in very high-HDI
countries. Both require deceased donors and highly specialised surgical teams.
Lung transplantation in particular demands sophisticated post-operative
immunosuppression management. The progression from kidney-only to multi-organ
transplantation serves as a proxy for the overall maturity of a country's
transplant system.


5. FINDING 4: POLICY MATTERS -- OVERPERFORMERS AND UNDERPERFORMERS
================================================================================

While HDI explains about 39% of the variance, the remaining 61% is driven by
policy choices, cultural factors, and institutional design.

5.1 Top overperformers (more donors than HDI predicts)

  Country    | HDI   | Donors PMP | Predicted | Residual
  -----------+-------+------------+-----------+---------
  Spain      | 0.918 | 49.4       | 14.9      | +26.0
  Portugal   | 0.890 | 36.8       | 13.3      | +16.0
  Croatia    | 0.889 | 29.0       | 13.3      | +13.1
  France     | 0.920 | 27.6       | 15.0      | +11.9
  Belgium    | 0.951 | 32.7       | 16.6      | +11.7
  Belarus    | 0.824 | 25.3       | 10.0      | +9.7
  Austria    | 0.930 | 20.9       | 15.5      | +9.2
  Czechia    | 0.915 | 28.5       | 14.7      | +9.2
  Italy      | 0.915 | 29.4       | 14.7      | +9.0

Spain is the standout case. The "Spanish Model" consists of several key policy
elements: opt-out (presumed consent) legislation, a dedicated three-tier
transplant coordination network (national, regional, hospital-level), trained
hospital-based donor coordinators in every major hospital, and no age limits on
donor acceptance.

Croatia, Portugal, and Italy have adopted elements of the Spanish Model with
notable success. Belarus achieves high rates despite lower HDI, likely driven
by a centralised healthcare system with strong institutional capacity for
donor identification.

5.2 Top underperformers (fewer donors than HDI predicts)

  Country    | HDI   | Donors PMP | Predicted | Residual
  -----------+-------+------------+-----------+---------
  Japan      | 0.925 | 1.2        | 15.2      | -13.8
  UAE        | 0.940 | 9.2        | 15.9      | -11.1
  Singapore  | 0.946 | 4.7        | 16.3      | -11.1
  Kazakhstan | 0.837 | 0.4        | 10.7      | -9.3
  Barbados   | 0.811 | 0.0        |  9.4      | -9.2
  Qatar      | 0.886 | 7.4        | 13.2      | -9.1
  Oman       | 0.858 | 0.4        | 11.8      | -9.1
  Georgia    | 0.844 | 0.0        | 11.1      | -8.7
  Albania    | 0.810 | 0.0        |  9.3      | -8.4

Japan is the most striking underperformer. Despite HDI 0.925, it achieves only
1.2 deceased donors per million. This is primarily attributed to cultural
attitudes toward brain death and bodily integrity. Japan's Organ Transplant Law
was only enacted in 1997, and for years required explicit written consent plus
family agreement. The law was revised in 2010 but cultural resistance remains.

Gulf states (UAE, Qatar, Oman) have high HDIs driven largely by oil wealth, but
their healthcare systems are still developing transplant infrastructure. These
cases demonstrate that wealth alone is not sufficient -- donation requires
specific institutional, legal, and cultural conditions.


6. FINDING 5: HDI DOES NOT PREDICT UTILISATION EFFICIENCY
================================================================================

We defined efficiency as Utilised DD / Actual DD and tested its correlation
with HDI.

6.1 Correlation coefficients

  Dataset                       | Pearson r | p-value | Spearman rho | p-value
  -------------------------------+-----------+---------+--------------+---------
  All country-years (n=808)     |  0.057    | 0.103   | -0.163       | < 0.001
  Latest year per country (n=88)| -0.002    | 0.989   | -0.265       | 0.012

There is no meaningful linear correlation. The Pearson r is essentially zero.
The Spearman rho is statistically significant but weak (-0.27), and
surprisingly negative -- meaning higher-HDI countries are slightly LESS
efficient.

6.2 Efficiency by HDI tier

  HDI tier    | Median efficiency | Mean efficiency | Observations
  ------------+-------------------+-----------------+-------------
  Low         | 100%              | 100%            |   1
  Medium      | 100%              |  95.1%          |  38
  High        |  99.3%            |  93.8%          | 194
  Very High   |  97.1%            |  95.2%          | 575

All tiers show very high median efficiency (above 97%). The slight decrease at
the top tier is a consequence of programme scale, not failure.


7. FINDING 6: DONOR VOLUME INVERSELY PREDICTS EFFICIENCY
================================================================================

Since HDI does not explain efficiency, we investigated what does.

7.1 Correlation coefficients

  Dataset                        | Pearson r | p-value | Spearman rho | p-value
  --------------------------------+-----------+---------+--------------+---------
  All country-years (n=896)      | -0.060    | 0.071   | -0.439       | < 0.001
  Latest year per country (n=89) | -0.099    | 0.357   | -0.491       | < 0.001

The Spearman rho of -0.49 (p < 0.001) confirms a moderate negative rank
correlation: countries with larger donor programmes tend to have lower
utilisation rates.

7.2 Illustrative examples

  Country        | Actual DD | Utilised DD | Efficiency | Programme size
  ---------------+-----------+-------------+------------+---------------
  United States  | 16,989    | 14,908      | 87.8%      | Very large
  Spain          |  2,562    |  2,278      | 88.9%      | Large
  Brazil         |  3,771    |  3,380      | 89.6%      | Large
  Italy          |  1,795    |  1,730      | 96.4%      | Large
  Canada         |    734    |    734      | 100%       | Medium
  Iran           |  1,016    |  1,000      | 98.4%      | Medium
  Bolivia        |      4    |      4      | 100%       | Small
  Iceland        |      3    |      3      | 100%       | Small
  Algeria        |      1    |      1      | 100%       | Minimal

7.3 Why this is not a problem

Lower efficiency in high-volume programmes is a natural consequence of scale:
  - Medical unsuitability: expanded donor criteria lead to more organs found
    unsuitable upon closer inspection.
  - Matching constraints: more donors means higher probability that specific
    organs cannot be matched in time.
  - Organ quality: high-volume programmes recover organs that smaller
    programmes might not even attempt, expanding the total pool but lowering
    the utilisation percentage.

Conclusion: HDI does not predict efficiency. Programme volume does, and
inversely. The bigger the donor pool, the lower the utilisation rate -- which
is actually a sign of a healthy, ambitious system.


8. SUMMARY OF ALL FINDINGS
================================================================================

  # | Finding                                        | Key metric              | Strength
  --+------------------------------------------------+-------------------------+---------------------
  1 | HDI predicts donation and transplant rates      | Spearman rho=0.75/0.77  | Strong, p < 0.001
  2 | Low-HDI countries rely on living donors (99.5%) | Living vs deceased %    | Structural pattern
  3 | Organ diversity increases with HDI              | Kidney: 92% -> 66%      | Structural pattern
  4 | Policy overrides HDI (Spain vs Japan)           | Regression residuals    | Notable outliers
  5 | HDI does not predict utilisation efficiency      | Pearson r = -0.002      | No correlation
  6 | Donor volume inversely predicts efficiency      | Spearman rho = -0.49    | Moderate, p < 0.001

Key takeaways for the paper:

1. Development is necessary but not sufficient for strong organ donation
   systems. The HDI correlation is strong, but outliers show that policy and
   culture matter enormously.

2. The living-to-deceased transition and organ diversification are two sides of
   the same coin: both require infrastructure that comes with higher
   development, and both dramatically expand transplant capacity.

3. Efficiency is not a useful metric for comparing countries of different sizes.
   It is an artefact of programme scale, not quality. The paper should frame
   lower utilisation in large programmes as evidence of ambitious donor
   recovery, not waste.

4. The Spanish Model provides a concrete, replicable policy template. Countries
   like Japan and Singapore demonstrate that even the highest development levels
   cannot overcome cultural barriers without targeted policy intervention.
